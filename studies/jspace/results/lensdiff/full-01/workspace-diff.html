How to read this

A language model writes one token at a time: a word or a piece of a word. At each token it holds an internal state, a long list of numbers. The J-lens, a method Anthropic published in July 2026, turns that state at a middle layer into a short list of words the model is poised to write later. We call the list the readout.

We took the answers the base model wrote to 500 MATH-500 problems and ran that same text through both models: the base, and the version AI2 trained with RL on math. At each of about million positions we compared the top 8 words of the two readouts. The text is identical, so every difference comes from the models.

What did not hold

  • The shift does not target the base model's failures. We expected a larger difference on the 65 problems only the RL model solves. It is smaller: points there against on the 398 both solve.
  • The readout is not an error detector. Asked which of the base's 500 answers are wrong, answer length alone scores (0.5 is chance, 1 is perfect). The best readout feature scores .
  • A reader's check agrees. Of 90 positions in 30 wrong answers where the RL readout stood out most, 10 sat on the first wrong step, 26 where a wrong value is reused, 20 on the wrong final answer, 21 on a correct step and 13 were unclear.
  • The lens is partly a next-word reader here. It passes its check only at layers 24 to 28 of 32 (it finds an upcoming word of the time, against for a mismatched window). At those layers its top word is the model's next word about half the time. It was fitted on the base model, on Wikipedia text.

What this points to

RL did not teach this model where its mistakes are. It changed a disposition: pause and reconsider more, conclude less. That matches the outside view, where the RL model's correct answers are twice as long as the base model's.

The next experiment acts on it. While the base model solves the problems, push its middle layers toward the hold-on words and away from the wrap-up words, and measure the MATH-500 score. Two controls decide whether the workspace matters: a random push of the same size, and the same preference applied directly to the output words.
