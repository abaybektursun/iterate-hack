### 2512.12895 — Wait, Wait, Wait... Why Do Reasoning Models Loop?, 2025
- Finding: Looping is a low-temperature effect of learning errors. Under greedy decoding, the share of looping generations (some 30-gram repeated at least 20 times) is 0.76 for R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B, 0.49 for 7B and 0.37 for 32B, and 0.04 for QwQ-32B. Looping falls as temperature rises from 0 to 1.0 for every model tested (Sec 2, App. A, Fig. 6, Table 1). In a synthetic graph task the paper finds two causes: risk aversion toward an easy cyclic action when the progress-making action is hard to learn, and a Transformer bias toward temporally correlated errors. Higher temperature reduces looping but leaves generations much longer than necessary (trained model mean length 67.4 vs 24.6 for a perfect learner, Sec 3.3).
- Where measured: open reasoning models (R1-Distill 1.5B/7B/32B/Llama-8B, OpenThinker3, QwQ-32B, Phi-4-reasoning) on AIME 2024+2025, 60 problems, 20 samples per problem and temperature, 30K-token cap, each model's default top-k/top-p, no repetition penalty. Top-p is never varied.

### 2601.05693 — Circular Reasoning: Understanding Self-Reinforcing Loops in Large Reasoning Models, 2026
- Finding: Loop rate falls with temperature but does not reach zero, and numerical loops barely respond to it. Total LoopBench loop rate at T 0.1 / 0.6 / 1.0: DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B 92.07 / 62.00 / 18.71 %; Qwen3-8B 35.57 / 29.29 / 10.71 %; Qwen3-8B-Base 42.29 / 22.29 / 20.15 %. Numerical loops on Qwen3-8B-Base: 38.86 / 20.57 / 19.86 % (Tables 1, 6, 7). The paper describes loop onset as a self-reinforcing collapse of entropy.
- Where measured: LoopBench and AIME25, with repetition penalty 1.1. Temperature was changed together with top-k (5/15/30) and top-p (0.95/0.9/0.9), so the effect of temperature alone is not isolated.

### 2610.24715 — When Do Repetition Loops Lock? Truncation Sampling, Bounded Penalties, and a Blind Spot in DRY, 2026
- Finding: Under top-p with threshold π > 1/2, a loop position becomes deterministic (escape probability exactly 0) once the loop token's probability reaches π. With temperature applied before top-p, at T 0.7 / top-p 0.9 the sufficient threshold falls from q ≥ 0.9 to q ≥ 0.823. Pure sampling never locks (Theorem 1, Prop. 3). In teacher-forced tests, 71–100 % of repeated units stay locked under top-p 0.9 vs 0 % under pure sampling (Table 2). Pure sampling still leaves a per-repetition escape probability of only 10^-1.5 to 10^-3.5 (App. B), so loops can still reach the length limit.
- Where measured: 11 open models, 150 repeated units per model (teacher-forced). Free-generation loop counts come from llama.cpp on structured generation tasks, not math.

### 2407.01082 — Turning Up the Heat: Min-p Sampling for Creative and Coherent LLM Outputs, 2024
- Finding: Min-p truncation scales the cutoff with the top token's probability, so it keeps chain-of-thought accuracy at temperatures where other samplers collapse. GSM8K CoT accuracy at τ = 1.0 / 1.5 / 2.0: temperature only 17.51 / 0.00 / 0.00; top-p 27.67 / 0.68 / 0.00; min-p 30.86 / 18.42 / 6.21. At τ = 0.7, min-p and top-p are similar (35.18 vs 36.09) (Table 2). Below τ = 1.5, all truncation methods perform similarly (Table 10). A follow-up (2506.13681) finds that min-p is indistinguishable from other samplers once hyperparameter-search volume is equalised.
- Where measured: Mistral-7B, GSM8K 8-shot CoT and GPQA Main 5-shot, vLLM.

### 1904.09751 — The Curious Case of Neural Text Degeneration, 2019
- Finding: Maximisation-based decoding degenerates into repetition because the model assigns high and rising probability to repeated text, while human text rarely stays in a high-probability zone. Repetition rate: greedy 73.66 %, beam 16 28.94 %, top-k 40 at t 0.7 8.86 %, pure sampling 0.22 %, nucleus p 0.95 0.36 %, human 0.28 % (Table 1). Per-token probability of a repeated phrase rises with each repetition (Fig. 4, Sec 4.3).
- Where measured: GPT-2 Large, 5,000 open-ended continuations of WebText prompts.

### 2206.02369 — Learning to Break the Loop: Analyzing and Mitigating Repetitions for Neural Text Generation, 2022
- Finding: Sentence-level repetition reinforces itself. The probability of repeating a sentence rises almost monotonically with the number of earlier repetitions in context, then levels off at a ceiling. Sentences with higher initial probability, such as those the model produced under maximisation decoding, reinforce faster (Sec 3, Figs. 2–3). Consecutive sentence-level repetition is 0.02 % of Wikitext-103 human text (Sec 1). Training on pseudo-repetitive data with a repetition penalty (DITTO) reduces loops without hurting perplexity.
- Where measured: GPT-2-style models and BART, Wikitext-103 and CNN/DailyMail. Metrics (TP, IP, WR) are computed on constructed repeated-sentence contexts.

### 2505.17813 — Don't Overthink it. Preferring Shorter Thinking Chains for Improved LLM Reasoning, 2025
- Finding: Within a question, shorter chains are more often correct. Across difficulty levels, harder questions use more tokens. Mean thinking tokens (thousands), correct / incorrect: LN-Super-49B easy 5.3 / 11.1, medium 11.4 / 17.1, hard 12.4 / 16.8; R1-670B hard 23.0 / 31.7. Hard problems use up to 2.9x the tokens of easy ones. Correct chains contain fewer backtracking words (R1-32B: 95 vs 352 per chain) (Sec 5.1, Table 2). The shortest of k chains is up to 34.5 % more accurate than the longest. short-3@k beats majority voting at every compute budget.
- Where measured: reasoning models (LN-Super-49B, R1-32B, R1-670B, QwQ) on AIME 2024/2025 and HMMT. Sampled chains with capped chains excluded. Reasoning models only, no base models.

### 2505.11711 — Reinforcement Learning Finetunes Small Subnetworks in Large Language Models, 2025
- Finding: In released bf16 checkpoints, RL leaves most weights unchanged (|Δ| ≤ 1e-5): 68.5 % for deepseek-math-7b-rl (GRPO), 86.0 % for DeepSeek-R1-Zero, 77.0 % for Eurus-2-7B-PRIME (Table 1). Yet the update matrices are 99.2–99.8 % of full rank and spread evenly over layers and matrix types (Table 2, Fig. 3). Sparsity depends on the equality tolerance: math-shepherd PPO is 52.45 % unchanged at 1e-8 vs 80.77 % at 1e-5 (App. A, Table 6). The authors attribute it to updates too small for the bf16 format (Sec 6, fn. 5). Rejection-sampling SFT is also sparse (91.2 %), while off-policy DPO is dense (6.8–7.7 %), so on-policy data rather than the RL objective tracks sparsity (Table 5).
- Where measured: public checkpoints from 7 algorithms and 10 models (7B–70B), each compared with its pre-RL checkpoint. One run per comparison.

### 2509.04259 — RL's Razor: Why Online Reinforcement Learning Forgets Less, 2025
- Finding: Among solutions to a new task, on-policy RL converges to the one closest in KL to the base model. Forward KL(base || fine-tuned) on new-task inputs predicts forgetting: quadratic fit R² = 0.96 on ParityMNIST and 0.71 for LLM runs. Weight-change L1 gives R² 0.34, Fisher-weighted L2 0.58, activation change 0.52–0.55 (Sec 4, Sec 6, Table 1, Fig. 11). On-policy 1-0 Reinforce behaves like GRPO and offline SFT like SimPO, so the cause is on-policy data, not negative gradients (Sec 5.1). Training in float32 gives the same performance with no update sparsity (Sec 6).
- Where measured: Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct (math, science Q&A, tool use), OpenVLA-7B robotics, and ParityMNIST with a 3-layer MLP (~500 runs per method). GRPO with no KL penalty.

### 2510.00553 — On Predictability of Reinforcement Learning Dynamics for Large Language Models, 2025
- Finding: The top singular direction of each matrix's RL update, rescaled to the full update's Frobenius norm, recovers close to 100 % of the RL accuracy (Sec 2.1–2.2, Fig. 3a, Table 8). Off-policy updates do not share this: rank-1 relative accuracy is 2.5 % for SFT and 1.5 % for offline distillation, versus 56.4 % for on-policy distillation and 62.25 % for DPO (App. C, Tables 7–8). Scaling the rank-1 update by λ from 0.1 to 1.0 raises MATH-500 quickly, then saturates, with a peak near λ ≈ 0.7 (Fig. 4b). The rank-1 direction evolves linearly over training: AlphaRL extrapolation from 40 % of training reaches an average of 53.31 vs 53.38 for full training (Table 1).
- Where measured: Qwen3-8B-Base and 7 other LLMs trained with PPO, RLOO, GRPO, Dr. GRPO and DAPO. Six math benchmarks, 32 samples per question, T 0.6. No random-direction control.

### 2610.37719 — What a Weight Update Buys: Leverage Across Training Stages, 2026
- Finding: Leverage is the Fisher quadratic form of an update divided by that of a random direction of the same norm. For five Olmo-3-7B RL-Zero deltas (Math, Code, IF, General, Mix) it is 388–1,508 (geometric mean 708), against 0.86 for pretraining windows and 5.5 for SFT (Sec 3, Table 1). A sign-flip null that keeps the magnitudes but randomises signs retains 354–1,226 of it, so the effect lies in signs and cross-coordinate structure, not in large magnitudes landing on high-Fisher coordinates (Table 2). RLVR leverage stays at 480–620 across 13 OLMo-2-1B checkpoints, while SFT and DPO trajectories lose leverage as they move (Sec 3–4, Table 3). Training in bf16 without an fp32 master copy freezes 36–99 % of coordinates.
- Where measured: released Olmo-3-7B, Tülu-3, Zephyr and OLMo-2 checkpoints, Fisher computed on a mixed 51k-token corpus. GRPO on Qwen2.5-0.5B (GSM8K, 150 steps).

### 2504.07986 — SEAL: Steerable Reasoning Calibration of Large Language Models for Free, 2025
- Finding: Reasoning hidden states separate into execution, reflection and transition thoughts, and excess reflection and transition correlate with failure. A single steering vector (mean of execution thoughts minus mean of reflection and transition thoughts, labelled by keywords such as "Wait", with no correctness labels) is added at each paragraph break. MATH-500 rises from 85.8 to 89.4 with tokens cut from 3,389 to 2,661 for R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, and from 67.0 to 78.0 (hard subset 54.2 to 68.3) for R1-Distill-1.5B. QwQ-32B gains only +0.6 (Table 1). Penalising the logits of "wait"-type words gives smaller gains (+1.6 to +9.6).
- Where measured: R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B and 7B, QwQ-32B-Preview. Greedy decoding. Vector from 1,000 MATH training problems at one layer (20 for 1.5B and 7B).

### 2510.07364 — Base Models Know How to Reason, Thinking Models Learn When, 2025
- Finding: In this paper's account, RL mainly teaches when to deploy reasoning behaviours the base model already has. Category steering vectors in the base, triggered where base and thinking model disagree, recover part of the gap. On MATH-500, Qwen2.5-7B goes from 63.9 to 75.7 against Open-Reasoner-Zero-7B's 87.6 (Sec 3.7, Table 1). Base and RL model disagree on only 5.9 % of generated tokens for this pair, against 12–24 % for SFT-distilled pairs (Tables 2, 5). Norm-matched random vectors give −28 % and −13 % of the gap on ORZ-1.5B and 32B, versus 77 % for the full pipeline (Sec 3.8, Fig. 4). Across nine pairs, about 76 % of RL gaps and 11 % of SFT gaps are recovered.
- Where measured: Qwen2.5 base vs Open-Reasoner-Zero 1.5B/7B/32B and other pairs. MATH-500 and GSM8K, 2,048-token budget, base greedy. The RL model is run at every token to trigger steering.

### 2603.22446 — Sparse but Critical: A Token-Level Analysis of Distributional Shifts in RLVR Fine-Tuning of LLMs, 2026
- Finding: Base and RL next-token distributions nearly coincide at more than 83 % (DAPO) and 98 % (SimpleRL) of positions (Sec 2.2, Fig. 2). Divergence is highest at the start of the response and rises again at the end. At high-divergence positions, over 80–90 % of RL top-1 tokens are in the base's top 3, but only about 30 % are already the base's top 1 (Sec 4.1–4.2). Sampling the RL token at those positions during base generation raises AIME24 from 8.23 to over 25 with 3.86 % of tokens (SimpleRL), and to over 44 with 7.8 % of tokens (DAPO) (Sec 3.2, Table 1). Swapping at random positions helps much less (App. A.6, Fig. 55).
- Where measured: Qwen2.5-32B base vs its DAPO and SimpleRL models, plus Qwen3-8B and Mistral-Small-24B. AIME 2024/2025 and GPQA-Diamond, 32 samples per problem, T 1.0, top-p 0.7.

### 2506.01939 — Beyond the 80/20 Rule: High-Entropy Minority Tokens Drive Effective Reinforcement Learning for LLM Reasoning, 2025
- Finding: Only a minority of CoT tokens have high entropy, and they act as forks between reasoning paths. RLVR largely keeps where these forks are and changes their entropy. Overlap of top-20 %-entropy positions between base and RL model is 98.9 % at step 16 and still 86.67 % at convergence (step 1360). Tokens with higher base entropy change more, and low-entropy tokens barely move (Sec 4, Table 1, Figs. 4–5). Restricting the policy gradient to the top 20 % of tokens by entropy matches full training on Qwen3-8B and beats it on 32B (+11.04 AIME'25), and lengthens responses (Sec 5, Table 2).
- Where measured: Qwen3-8B/14B/32B base, DAPO RLVR, 16 samples per question on six math benchmarks, maximum length 28,672.

### 2610.30882 — Inherited, Not Invented: Tracing the Provenance of Wait in Reasoning Models, 2026
- Finding: Zero-RL multiplies an existing "wait" marker rather than creating it. Across 18 zero-RL runs from bases without the word, MATH-500 rises by a median of 42.9 points while "wait" stays below 0.05 per 1k tokens in 17 of 18 (Sec 4). In Olmo-3-1025-7B, "wait" appears at ≤ 0.04 per 1k tokens in pretraining stage 1, 4.03 after mid-training (88.6 % sentence-initial) and 4.94 in the base. Olmo-3-7B-RL-Zero-Math reaches a peak of 17.0 at step 1900 and 12.4 at release, at T 1.0 (Table 2). At T 0.6 the RL-Zero model writes 76.5 per 1k vs 5.1 for the base, and 29.1 % of its responses hit the 8,192-token limit vs 17.8 % for the base (Table 14). On injected-error prefixes, RL makes "wait" more sensitive and more liberal: d′ 0.57 → 1.09, following 73.7 % of error prefixes (base 47.1 %) and 25.4 % of error-free ones (base 19.9 %) (Sec 4.1, Fig. 4).
- Where measured: OLMo-3 and MiMo lineages plus 18 zero-RL runs. MATH-500, 500 problems x 2 samples, 6 decoding seeds. Observational.

### 2504.13837 — Does Reinforcement Learning Really Incentivize Reasoning Capacity in LLMs Beyond the Base Model?, 2025
- Finding: RLVR raises pass@1 by concentrating on paths the base model already samples, while coverage at large k does not grow. Per-problem MATH-500 coverage at 128 samples: both models solve 92.4 %, base only 3.6 %, RL only 1.0 % (all solved by the base within 1,024 samples), neither 3.0 %. On AIME24 (1,024 samples) no problem is solved by RL only (Sec 4.1, Table 2). Six RL algorithms reach pass@1 of 73.5–75.6 vs the base's 34.5, and pass@256 of 96.4–97.4 vs the base's 96.2 (App. C.5, Table 3).
- Where measured: Qwen2.5-7B base vs SimpleRL-Zoo GRPO and six algorithms trained on 2,000 Omni-MATH problems. T 0.6, top-p 0.95, 16,384-token cap.

### 2611.10307 — How Much Is Left Matters: False Positives and False Negatives in Partial-Prompt Contamination Tests, 2026
- Finding: Partial-prompt contamination flags depend heavily on how many words are left to complete. MATH-500 raw flag rate at a 60 % prefix: 31.5 % vs 5.5 % for leftovers of ≤ 8 vs ≥ 25 words (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct). 49–73 % of all flags come from leftovers of ≤ 8 words (Sec 4.1, Tables 1, 3). On true negatives (AIME 2025/2026), exact match at an 80 % prefix flags 8.6 % (Sec 4.2). After cross-family calibration, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct is flagged on 19.6 % of MATH-500 vs 6.0 % for Olmo-3-7B-Instruct (Sec 4.4). A 15-word window of 52.9 % of MATH-500 problems occurs in the OLMo-2 training corpus, but Olmo-3's flags do not track that exposure (Sec 4.5).
- Where measured: seven instruction-tuned or distilled models (Qwen2.5, R1-Distill, Qwen3, Olmo-3-7B-Instruct). Greedy completion of 256 tokens, ROUGE-L ≥ 0.75 or exact match. Infini-gram search of the OLMo-2 corpus.

### 2503.20783 — Understanding R1-Zero-Like Training: A Critical Perspective, 2025
- Finding: GRPO's per-response length normalisation makes incorrect long responses less penalised. Its per-question std normalisation over-weights very easy and very hard questions. Together these make response length keep growing even after reward stops improving. Dr. GRPO removes both terms and reduces the length of incorrect responses (Sec 3.1–3.2, Fig. 5). Base-model scores depend heavily on the prompt template: on MATH-500, Qwen2.5-Math-7B scores 0.0 with the R1 template, 50.6 with the Qwen template and 69.0 with no template (Sec 2, table "Base model + Template").
- Where measured: Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B/7B, Llama-3.2-3B and DeepSeek-V3-Base. MATH training questions, five math benchmarks, greedy decoding, 3,000-token budget.

### 2508.08221 — Part I: Tricks or Traps? A Deep Dive into RL for LLM Reasoning, 2025
- Finding: Overlong filtering (masking the reward of truncated responses) depends on the length cap. With an 8k-token threshold it gives substantial gains and shorter responses. With a 20k threshold the benefit largely disappears, models trained with filtering write longer responses, and both positive and negative samples are clipped more often because of repetitive or non-terminating outputs (Sec 4.4, Fig. 13). Across the study, advantage normalisation (group mean with batch std) and token-level loss aggregation alone ("Lite PPO") beat GRPO and DAPO on a critic-free policy.
- Where measured: Qwen3-8B-Base and Qwen3-8B as initial policies, six math benchmarks, several maximum generation lengths.

### 2505.22617 — The Entropy Mechanism of Reinforcement Learning for Reasoning Language Models, 2025
- Finding: Without intervention, policy entropy drops sharply early in RL and performance saturates. Accuracy follows R = −a·exp(H) + b, fitted with 2 coefficients across more than 200 points. 73 % of entropy loss and 76 % of the performance gain occur in the first 200 of 2,400 steps (Sec 2, Figs. 2–4). Entropy change is driven by the covariance between action log-probability and advantage, which stays mostly positive. Limiting updates on the few high-covariance tokens (KL-Cov) keeps entropy over 10x higher and adds 2.0 points on average at 7B and 6.4 at 32B (Sec 3–4, Table 2).
- Where measured: 11 models (Qwen2.5, Mistral, LLaMA, DeepSeek families) under GRPO on math and code. KL-Cov on Qwen2.5-7B/32B base.

### 2506.10947 — Spurious Rewards: Rethinking Training Signals in RLVR, 2025
- Finding: On Qwen2.5-Math-7B, rewards with no correctness signal give most of the RLVR gain. MATH-500 rises from 49.4 by +21.4 points with random reward, +24.1 with incorrect labels and +29.1 with ground truth. AIME25 gains under spurious rewards are −0.4 to +4.5 (Sec 2.2, Fig. 1). The gain comes from eliciting a pre-existing behaviour: code reasoning without an interpreter rises from 65 % to about 90 % of responses within 15 steps, and 58.3 % of the gain comes from problems that switch to code (Sec 5, Table 1). OLMo-2-7B and Llama models stay flat or lose under spurious rewards and gain mainly with ground truth (Sec 3, Fig. 3).
- Where measured: Qwen2.5-Math-7B/1.5B, Qwen2.5-7B, OLMo-2-1124-7B (+SFT), Llama-3.x. GRPO with no KL, lr 5e-7, 16 rollouts, 300 steps, DeepScaleR prompts. MATH-500 greedy.

### 2504.20571 — Reinforcement Learning for Reasoning in Large Language Models with One Training Example, 2025
- Finding: RLVR on a single training example raises Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B on MATH-500 from 36.0 % to 73.6 % (the same as the 1.2k-example set; 8.6 points beyond format correction). Two examples reach 74.8 % (Sec 1, Fig. 1). Test accuracy keeps rising after training accuracy on the example saturates ("post-saturation generalisation"). Overfitting appears only after about 1.4k steps, and test responses stay readable even when the training example's output turns to gibberish (Sec 3.2, Figs. 2–3). The effect comes mainly from the policy-gradient loss. Adding entropy loss raises the post-saturation gain by 2.3 points on average.
- Where measured: Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B/7B, Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B. GRPO and PPO, examples ranked by historical reward variance, six math benchmarks.

### 2609.18587 — Label-free steering: Compressing test-time reinforcement learning into bias-only subspaces, 2026
- Finding: Majority-vote pseudo-label RL captures less of the gain than gold-label RL. On MATH-500, Qwen2.5-7B base goes from 45.9 to 76.67 with vote-reward GRPO on about 100K MLP bias parameters, to 77.00 with vote-reward full fine-tuning, and to 87.93 with gold-label full fine-tuning. ### 2504.07086 — A Sober Look at Progress in Language Model Reasoning: Pitfalls and Paths to Reproducibility, 2025
- Finding: Single-run reasoning scores are noisy enough to hide or fake RL gains. Pass@1 varies by 5 to 15 points across 20 seeds on small sets (AIME'24 up to 15, AMC'23 up to 13), and averaging over about 30 seeds is needed to stabilise (Sec 3.2, Figs. 2–3). Hardware alone moves AIME'24 by up to 8 % (OpenRS-1.5B) and 6 % (R1-Distill-7B). With deterministic CUDA, OpenThinker2-7B still scores 53.0 ± 4.6 on A100 vs 57.1 ± 5.2 on H100. Consecutive runs on the same cluster differ (19.3–23.0 %) (Sec 3.3, Fig. 10). Re-evaluated, most RL methods give modest gains and SFT generalises more consistently.
- Where measured: nine 1.5B/7B reasoning models (R1-Distill, DeepScaleR, OpenRS, S1.1, OpenThinker). AIME'24/'25, AMC'23, MATH-500, lighteval + vLLM, A100/H100 clusters.

### 2505.22203 — From Accuracy to Robustness: A Study of Rule- and Model-based Verifiers in Mathematical Reasoning, 2025
- Finding: Rule-based math verifiers are precise (> 99 %) but miss correctly formatted variants. Average recall is 86 % (14 % of correct answers rejected) and drops to 0.78 on Skywork-OR1. Recall falls as the generating model gets stronger: long-CoT models average 0.92, lower than weaker models (Sec 3, Figs. 2–3, Table 5). Model-based verifiers raise static recall (84 → 92 % on Skywork-OR1) but are hacked during RL. With a fine-tuned verifier, training reward jumps around step 450 while evaluation does not improve (55.6 vs 55.0, Table 2) or falls (58.7 → 55.5) (Sec 4).
- Where measured: HuggingFace Math-Verify, Qwen-Math and other verifiers on responses from models up to R1-Distill-Qwen-32B. Datasets include DeepScaleR, ORZ-Math and Skywork-OR1. RL on Qwen2.5 models.

### 2503.18892 — SimpleRL-Zoo: Investigating and Taming Zero Reinforcement Learning for Open Base Models in the Wild, 2025
- Finding: Zero-RL gains and behaviours depend on the base family. MATH-500, base → zero-RL: Mistral-7B 4.2 → 15.8, Llama-3.1-8B 13.6 → 23.0, DeepSeek-Math-7B 19.4 → 39.6, Mistral-Small-24B 43.6 → 70.6, Qwen2.5-7B 64.6 → 78.2, Qwen2.5-Math-7B 63.6 → 80.2 (Table 1). A strict format reward (−1 if no \boxed{}) penalises correct explorations of base models that cannot yet follow the format. Llama-3.1-8B grows longer responses with no accuracy gain and collapses, and Qwen2.5-7B later loses accuracy and CoT length (Sec 3.1, Fig. 6). ### 1908.10090 — On NMT Search Errors and Model Errors: Cat Got Your Tongue?, 2019
- Finding: In neural machine translation the exact mode of the model is often degenerate, and approximate search works because it fails to find it. Under exact search, the global best translation is the empty string for 51.8 % of sentences, and BLEU falls to 2.1 from 29.3 (greedy) and 30.3 (beam 10). Greedy and beam 10 make search errors on 73.6 % and 57.7 % of sentences (Sec 3, Table 1). The empty mode affects almost every source sentence longer than 40 tokens (Fig. 4). It persists across LSTM, SliceNet and Transformer-Big (25.8 % empty) (Table 2). A minimum-length constraint still leaves a peak of too-short outputs. Length normalisation fixes length but does not reach beam BLEU (Sec 4, Table 4).
- Where measured: Transformer-base trained on WMT18 En-De, WMT15 news-test (2,169 sentences), exact depth-first search.

### 2109.01903 — Robust fine-tuning of zero-shot models, 2021
- Finding: Linearly interpolating between the weights of a pretrained model and its fine-tuned version, (1−α)θ0 + αθ1, beats both endpoints over a range of α. At α = 0.5 it is near-optimal across experiments, improves distribution-shift accuracy by 4–6 points over prior work and raises ImageNet accuracy by 1.6. On six further shifts it gains 3.5, 6.2, 1.7, 2.1, 9.0 and 23.2 points over the fine-tuned model while losing at most 0.3 on the reference distribution (Sec 3, Table 1, App. B, C.2).
- Where measured: CLIP ViT and ResNet backbones (and ALIGN, BASIC) fine-tuned end-to-end on ImageNet. Evaluated on ImageNet-V2/R/Sketch/A/ObjectNet, WILDS, CIFAR-10.1/10.2 and video-robustness sets.
